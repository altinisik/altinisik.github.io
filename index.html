N. Ezgi Altınışık

Assistant Professor, Department of Anthropology, Hacettepe University

I am an assistant professor in the Department of Anthropology at Hacettepe University, where I co-lead the Human_G research group. As a paleogenomicist and bioarchaeologist, my work sits at the intersection of ancient genomics, historical demography, and archaeological inquiry.

My research focuses on unraveling ancient population dynamics, human mobility, and kinship structures, with a primary geographic focus on Anatolia and the broader Mediterranean world. By combining high-resolution archaeogenomic data, radiocarbon modeling, and statistical/computational approaches, I explore how societal transitions, mating systems, and mobility shaped human diversity through time.

Research Interests: Ancient DNA (aDNA) • Paleogenomics • Kinship & Mating Systems (Runs of Homozygosity) • Population Genetics • Bioarchaeology • Mediterranean & Anatolian Prehistory

  1. C-Turkey: A Comprehensive Radiocarbon Dataset from Türkiye
    N. Ezgi Altınışık
    Journal of Open Archaeology Data, Aug 2026
    Abs DOI Bib

    Anatolia serves as a pivotal geographic and cultural crossroad. However, its radiocarbon record has remained fragmented across international databases and local literature. Here, I present the C-Turkey Dataset, a curated repository comprising 3,390 radiocarbon dates, including 885 dates previously absent from global datasets. A distinctive feature of this dataset is its systematic focus on human skeletal remains. By providing detailed annotations for directly dated skeletons, where applicable, linked ancient DNA (aDNA) identifiers. C-Turkey bridges the gap between chronometric data and bioarchaeological research. This integration prevents redundant sampling of finite biological remains and facilitates high-resolution population history reconstructions. Three key approaches were employed in dataset preparation: i) computational extraction and cleaning using the C14bazaar tool, ii) if possible, manual cross-verification of original primary sources to ensure data integrity, and iii) the systematic inclusion of recent literature and Turkish-language publications often inaccessible to the global scientific community. As an open-access resource, C-Turkey promotes academic equity and provides a robust chronometric foundation for the multi-disciplinary study of the Near East.

  2. A Genomic Snapshot of Demographic and Cultural Dynamism in Upper Mesopotamia during the Neolithic Transition
    N. Ezgi Altınışık, Duygu Deniz Kazancı, Ayça Aydoğan, Hasan Can Gemici, Ömür Dilek Erdal, Savaş Sarıaltun, Kıvılcım Başak Vural, Dilek Koptekin, Kanat Gürün, Ekin Sağlıcan, and 15 more authors
    Science Advances, Nov 2022
    Abs DOI Bib

    Upper Mesopotamia played a key role in the Neolithic Transition in Southwest Asia through marked innovations in symbolism, technology, and diet. We present 13 ancient genomes (c. 8500 to 7500 cal BCE) from Pre-Pottery Neolithic Çayönü in the Tigris basin together with bioarchaeological and material culture data. Our findings reveal that Çayönü was a genetically diverse population, carrying mixed ancestry from western and eastern Fertile Crescent, and that the community received immigrants. Our results further suggest that the community was organized along biological family lines. We document bodily interventions such as head shaping and cauterization among the individuals examined, reflecting Çayönü’s cultural ingenuity. Last, we identify Upper Mesopotamia as the likely source of eastern gene flow into Neolithic Anatolia, in line with material culture evidence. We hypothesize that Upper Mesopotamia’s cultural dynamism during the Neolithic Transition was the product not only of its fertile lands but also of its interregional demographic connections.

  4. Palaeo-Eskimo Genetic Ancestry and the Peopling of Chukotka and North America
    Pavel Flegontov, N. Ezgi Altınışık, Piya Changmai, Nadin Rohland, Swapan Mallick, Nicole Adamski, Deborah A. Bolnick, Nasreen Broomandkhoshbacht, Francesca Candilio, Brendan J. Culleton, and 25 more authors
    Nature, Jun 2019
    Abs DOI Bib

    Much of the American Arctic was first settled 5,000 years ago, by groups of people known as Palaeo-Eskimos. They were subsequently joined and largely displaced around 1,000 years ago by ancestors of the present-day Inuit and Yup’ik. The genetic relationship between Palaeo-Eskimos and Native American, Inuit, Yup’ik and Aleut populations remains uncertain. Here we present genomic data for 48 ancient individuals from Chukotka, East Siberia, the Aleutian Islands, Alaska, and the Canadian Arctic. We co-analyse these data with data from present-day Alaskan Iñupiat and West Siberian populations and published genomes. Using methods based on rare-allele and haplotype sharing, as well as established techniques, we show that Palaeo-Eskimo-related ancestry is ubiquitous among people who speak Na-Dene and Eskimo-Aleut languages. We develop a comprehensive model for the Holocene peopling events of Chukotka and North America, and show that Na-Dene-speaking peoples, people of the Aleutian Islands, and Yup’ik and Inuit across the Arctic region all share ancestry from a single Palaeo-Eskimo-related Siberian source.
