My doctoral research at the University of Ostrava, *Bridging the Americas and Eurasia: Population History of American Arctic Peoples*, explored how Siberia and the Americas were connected through the movements of people over the last several thousand years.

The American Arctic was first settled around 5,000 years ago by groups known as Palaeo-Eskimos, who were later joined and largely replaced by the ancestors of present-day Inuit and Yup'ik. How Palaeo-Eskimos relate to present-day Arctic and Native American peoples had long remained uncertain. Analysing genomic data from 48 ancient individuals from Chukotka, East Siberia, the Aleutian Islands, Alaska and the Canadian Arctic, we found that Palaeo-Eskimo-related ancestry is present in all peoples speaking Na-Dene and Eskimo-Aleut languages, and developed a comprehensive model of the Holocene peopling of Chukotka and North America in which these groups share ancestry from a single Siberian source {% cite Flegontov2019-aj %}.

Earlier, a genomic study of the Kets of the Yenisei River basin, often described as the last nomadic hunter-gatherers of Siberia, showed that they belong to a cluster of Siberian populations most closely related to Palaeo-Eskimos and carry a high degree of ancestry related to the Upper Palaeolithic Mal'ta individual, with implications for the proposed linguistic link between Ket and the Na-Dene languages of North America {% cite Flegontov2016-my %}.

### Side project

Alongside my doctoral work, I contributed to a study of Indian genetic heritage in mainland Southeast Asia, which detected low levels of South Asian ancestry in Thai populations with historical connections to ancient Indian-influenced states, suggesting that migration played a role in the spread of Indian culture in the region {% cite Changmai2022-bm %}.
